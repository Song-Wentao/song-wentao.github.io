## Moving Beyond Single Species

*   So far, we've focused on dynamics *within* a single species (intraspecific competition).
*   Now, we consider interactions *between* different species.
*   **Interspecific Competition:** Competition between individuals of *different* species for a shared, limiting resource.

---

## Shared Limited Resources

*   What can species compete for?
    *   **Food:** Lions and hyenas competing for a zebra carcass.
    *   **Space:** Barnacles competing for space on a rocky shore.
    *   **Water:** Desert shrubs competing for water.
    *   **Light:** Trees in a forest competing for sunlight.
    *   **Nesting Sites:** Birds competing for tree holes.

---

## Competitive Exclusion Principle

*   **Gause's Principle:** Two species competing for the *exact same* limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely.
*   The competitively superior species will eventually eliminate the inferior one from that habitat.
*   For coexistence to occur, species must have some form of **resource partitioning**.

---

## Mechanisms of Coexistence: Niche Differentiation

*   The **ecological niche** is the sum of a species' use of biotic and abiotic resources.
*   **Resource Partitioning:** Natural selection drives competing species to use resources in different ways.
    *   **Spatial:** Feeding/ living in different areas.
    *   **Temporal:** Being active at different times (day vs. night).
    *   **Morphological:** Evolving different tools (e.g., beak size in birds).

---

## Case Study: Darwin's Finches

*   On the Galápagos Islands, finch species have different beak sizes and shapes.
*   **Geospiza fortis:** Medium ground finch - medium-sized seeds.
*   **Geospiza fuliginosa:** Small ground finch - small seeds.
*   **Geospiza magnirostris:** Large ground finch - large, hard seeds.
*   **This niche differentiation reduces competition and allows coexistence.**

---

## Modeling Competition: Lotka-Volterra

*   Extends the logistic growth model to include a second species.
*   Equations:
    *   $ \frac{dN_1}{dt} = r_1N_1 \left(1 - \frac{N_1 + \alpha_{12}N_2}{K_1}\right) $
    *   $ \frac{dN_2}{dt} = r_2N_2 \left(1 - \frac{N_2 + \alpha_{21}N_1}{K_2}\right) $
*   **Key new parameters:**
    *   $ \alpha_{12} $: **Competition coefficient.** Effect of species 2 on species 1.
    *   $ \alpha_{21} $: Effect of species 1 on species 2.

---

## Interpreting the Competition Coefficients

*   $ \alpha_{12} = 0.5 $ means that **one individual of species 2** uses the shared resources as much as **0.5 individuals of species 1**.
*   It converts individuals of the competitor species into "equivalent" individuals of one's own species.

---

## Four Possible Outcomes

1.  **Species 1 wins:** If its competition is too strong ($\alpha_{21} > K_2/K_1$ and $\alpha_{12} < K_1/K_2$).
2.  **Species 2 wins:** If its competition is too strong.
3.  **Stable Coexistence:** If intraspecific competition is stronger than interspecific competition for *both* species. This is the mathematical basis for niche differentiation.
4.  **Unstable Equilibrium:** The winner depends on who started with more individuals.

---

## Summary

*   Interspecific competition is a major force structuring ecological communities.
*   The **Competitive Exclusion Principle** predicts that complete competitors cannot coexist.
*   **Niche differentiation / resource partitioning** is the primary pathway to stable coexistence.
*   Models like Lotka-Volterra help us formalize these ideas.
