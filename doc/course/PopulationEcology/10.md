## A Pervasive Interaction

*   **Parasitism:** A +/- interaction where one organism (the **parasite**) benefits by deriving nutrients at the expense of another organism (the **host**).
*   Extremely common; likely every species is a host to at least one parasite.

---

## Parasites vs. Predators

| Feature | Predator | Parasite |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Number of prey/hosts** | Many | One or few |
| **Fate of prey/host** | Killed immediately | Usually not killed (or killed slowly) |
| **Size relative to prey** | Larger | Smaller |
| **Interaction time** | Short | Long |

---

## Types of Parasites

*   **Microparasites:** Small, multiply within host (e.g., viruses, bacteria, protozoa). Short generation time.
*   **Macroparasites:** Larger, do not multiply directly within host (e.g., ticks, tapeworms, fleas). Often have complex life cycles.
*   **Parasitoids:** An insect (e.g., wasp) whose larva consumes the host alive, eventually killing it. Intermediate between predator and parasite.

---

## Key Concepts in Parasitism

*   **Virulence:** The harm caused by a parasite to its host.
    *   High virulence: often kills host (e.g., Ebola).
    *   Low virulence: host survives (e.g., common cold virus).
*   **Transmission:** How the parasite spreads to new hosts (e.g., direct contact, vectors like mosquitoes, contaminated water).

---

## The Virulence-Transmission Trade-Off

*   A central hypothesis in disease ecology.
*   **High Virulence:** Parasite uses host resources aggressively → high replication → **high transmission rate**. BUT it may kill the host quickly, cutting off the resource.
*   **Low Virulence:** Parasite is less harmful → host lives longer → **longer transmission period**. BUT transmission rate may be lower.
*   Natural selection often favors an **intermediate level of virulence**.

---

## S-I-R Models

*   Simple epidemiological models that track host populations.
*   **S:** Susceptible individuals.
*   **I:** Infected (and infectious) individuals.
*   **R:** Removed individuals (recovered with immunity, or dead).
*   The model tracks flows between these compartments (S -> I -> R).

---

## Density-Dependent Transmission

*   The rate of new infections often depends on host density.
*   **Example:** The "mass action" assumption: new infections = $\beta S I$, where $\beta$ is the transmission rate.
*   **This makes disease a density-dependent regulating factor.** Diseases can spread rapidly in dense populations but may fade out in sparse ones.

---

## Case Study: Myxoma Virus & Rabbits

*   Revisited from Lecture 4.
*   Virus introduced to control rabbits in Australia.
*   **Initially:** Extremely high virulence (>99% mortality).
*   **Over time:** Natural selection favored **lower virulence strains**.
    *   Why? A virus that killed its host instantly had no time to be transmitted to a new host by mosquitoes.
    *   Less virulent strains persisted because their hosts lived longer, allowing for more transmission.

---

## Coevolutionary Arms Race

*   Hosts evolve defenses (immune system, behavioral avoidance).
*   Parasites evolve to overcome defenses (antigenic variation, immunosuppression).
*   This can lead to a **Red Queen effect**: hosts and parasites must constantly evolve just to maintain their current level of defense/offense.

---

## Importance of Parasites

*   **Population Regulation:** Can act as a potent density-dependent factor.
*   **Conservation:** Introduced diseases can devastate naive populations (e.g., avian malaria in Hawaiian birds).
*   **Community Ecology:** Parasites can indirectly affect competition and food webs.

---

## Summary

*   Parasitism is a +/- interaction with a vast diversity of forms.
*   **Virulence** is a key trait shaped by an evolutionary trade-off with **transmission**.
*   Disease dynamics can be modeled and are often **density-dependent**.
*   Host-parasite interactions are a classic example of coevolution.
