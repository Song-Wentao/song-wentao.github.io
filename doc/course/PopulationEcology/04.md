## What is Population Regulation?

*   The process by which density-dependent factors **stabilize** a population around a carrying capacity (K) or cause it to fluctuate within a bounded range.
*   It implies a **feedback loop**: population size affects factors that, in turn, affect future population size.

---

## Density-Dependence vs. Density-Independence

| Feature | Density-Dependent | Density-Independent |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Effect** | Depends on population size (N) | Does not depend on N |
| **Mechanism** | **Biotic factors** (competition, predation, disease) | **Abiotic factors** (weather, fire, hurricanes) |
| **Role** | **Regulates** population | Causes **unpredictable** changes |

---

## Common Density-Dependent Factors

1.  **Competition:** For food, shelter, mates (Intraspecific - Lecture 3).
2.  **Predation:** Predators may focus on a common prey species.
3.  **Parasitism & Disease:** Spreads more easily in dense populations.
4.  **Waste Accumulation:** Toxins build up (e.g., yeast in a vial).
5.  **Emigration:** Individuals may leave a crowded area.

---

## Case Study: Lynx and Snowshoe Hare

*   **Classic example of predator-prey regulation (more in Lecture 11).**
*   **Cycle:**
    1.  Hare population ↑ (lots of food).
    2.  Lynx population ↑ (lots of prey).
    3.  Hare population ↓ (heavy predation).
    4.  Lynx population ↓ (starvation).
    5.  Repeat.
*   **This is regulation:** Predation (density-dependent) causes cyclical fluctuations around an average.

---

## Case Study: Disease Regulation

*   **Example: Myxomatosis virus and rabbits in Australia.**
*   Rabbits were an invasive pest, growing exponentially.
*   Virus was introduced.
*   **In high-density populations:** Virus spread rapidly, causing massive die-offs (highly density-dependent).
*   Virus acted as a regulating factor, drastically reducing rabbit numbers.

---

## Density-Independent Factors Can Interact

*   A density-independent event can *create conditions* for density-dependent regulation.
*   **Example:** A hurricane (density-independent) blows down trees in a forest.
    *   This creates open sunlit patches.
    *   Saplings now compete *density-dependently* for this newly available light and space.

---

## Delayed Density Dependence

*   The effect of population density isn't always immediate.
*   **Example:**
    *   High density of herbivores one year → they overgraze plants.
    *   The *next year*, plant biomass is low → herbivores starve.
*   This delay can cause populations to **overshoot** K and then crash, potentially leading to cycles.

---

## Do All Populations Show Regulation?

*   Not all populations are stable.
*   Some show large, erratic fluctuations driven mostly by density-independent factors (e.g., insects affected by weather).
*   The question of "what regulates populations?" is a central and sometimes debated topic in ecology.

---

## Key Takeaway

*   **Regulation** is the result of **density-dependent feedback**.
*   It prevents unlimited growth and often leads to populations fluctuating within a predictable range.
*   Real populations are influenced by a **complex interplay** of both density-dependent and density-independent factors.
