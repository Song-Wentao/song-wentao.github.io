# Lecture 04: The Genetics of Populations
**Textbook Chapter:** Population Genetics
**Key Concepts:** Quantifying Selection, Fitness, Adaptive Landscapes, Genetic Constraints

---

## 1. From Description to Prediction
*   Population genetics provides the **mathematical framework** for evolutionary theory.
*   It allows us to **quantify** the strength of evolutionary forces and make predictions about how traits will change.

---

## 2. Quantifying Fitness and Selection
*   **Fitness (w):** The relative contribution of an individual or genotype to the next generation.
    *   Survival + Reproduction = Evolutionary Success.
    *   By convention, the highest fitness in a population is set to w = 1.
*   **Selection Coefficient (s):** The relative intensity of selection *against* a genotype. s = 1 - w.
    *   *Example:* If a genotype has a fitness of 0.7, its selection coefficient is s = 0.3. It is selected against.

---

## 3. Models of Selection
*   **Directional Selection:** The change in allele frequency per generation can be predicted mathematically.
*   **Heterozygote Advantage (Overdominance):** The heterozygote has higher fitness than either homozygote (e.g., Sickle Cell Anemia). **Maintains genetic variation.**
*   **Heterozygote Disadvantage (Underdominance):** The heterozygote has lower fitness. Unstable, can lead to rapid speciation.

---

## 4. The Adaptive Landscape
*   A powerful metaphor developed by Sewall Wright.
*   **X & Y Axes:** Represent combinations of alleles or traits.
*   **Z Axis (Height):** Represents the average fitness of a population with that combination.
*   **Peaks:** Represent combinations with high fitness (adaptive optima).
*   **Valleys:** Represent combinations with low fitness.
*   **Natural selection** pushes populations **up** the nearest fitness peak.

---

## 5. Why Populations Aren't Perfect: Constraints on Adaptation
*   **Lack of Genetic Variation:** Selection can only act on existing variation. You can't select for wings if the genes for wings don't exist.
*   **Pleiotropy:** A single gene affects multiple traits. A change that helps one trait might hurt another.
*   **Genetic Linkage:** Genes located close together on a chromosome are inherited together. A beneficial allele might be linked to a harmful one, slowing its spread.
*   **Historical Constraints:** Evolution works on what is already there, not from scratch. (e.g., The panda's "thumb" is a modified wrist bone, not an ideal design).
*   **Trade-offs:** Resources allocated to one function (e.g., reproduction) cannot be allocated to another (e.g., defense).

---

## 6. Example: The Peppered Moth (Biston betularia)
*   **A classic study in quantitative population genetics.**
*   **Pre-industrial revolution:** Light-colored moths were camouflaged on lichen-covered trees. w(light) ~1, w(dark) ~0.
*   **Post-industrial revolution:** Soot darkened trees. Dark moths now camouflaged. Selection reversed! w(dark) > w(light).
*   **Kettlewell's experiments:** Showed that bird predation was the agent of selection and measured the strength of selection (s).
