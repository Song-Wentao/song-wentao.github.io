### **Part 1: Review & The Dimension of Time (10 min)**

*   **From Last Time:** We've explored the main species interactions (competition, predation) that structure communities.
*   **Today's Focus:** How do these interactions play out **over time**? Communities are not static; they change.
*   **Key Concept: Ecological Succession** – the predictable process of change in species composition following a disturbance.

---

### **Part 2: Patterns of Succession (25 min)**

#### **1. Primary Succession**
*   **Starts on:** **Lifeless substrate** with no organic soil (e.g., bare rock after glacial retreat, volcanic lava flow).
*   **Pioneer Species:** Lichens and mosses that can weather rock and begin soil formation.
*   **Process:** Extremely slow (centuries to millennia), as soil must be built from scratch.

#### **2. Secondary Succession**
*   **Starts on:** **Existing soil** where a community has been removed (e.g., after a forest fire, hurricane, or agricultural abandonment).
*   **Pioneer Species:** Fast-growing, sun-loving, weedy annual plants.
*   **Process:** Much faster than primary succession (decades to centuries).

#### **The General Pattern:**
1.  **Colonization** by pioneer species.
2.  **Facilitation:** Early species modify the environment (e.g., add organic matter, provide shade), making it more suitable for later species.
3.  **Replacement:** Later species outcompete earlier ones.
4.  **Climax Community:** A (theoretically) stable, self-replacing endpoint.

---

### **Part 3: Mechanisms Driving Succession (25 min)**

*   **Facilitation (++):** Early species *help* later species establish (e.g., fixing nitrogen, providing shade for seedlings).
*   **Inhibition (-):** Early species *hinder* the establishment of later species (e.g., via competition or allelopathy - releasing chemicals). Succession proceeds as organisms die or are disturbed.
*   **Tolerance (0):** Later species are simply those that can *tolerate* the conditions and outcompete others over the long term. They don't require help from pioneers.

**Most successional sequences involve a mix of these three mechanisms.**

---

### **Part 4: Is There an Endpoint? The Climax Community Debate (20 min)**

*   **Clementsian View:** A deterministic, stable endpoint in equilibrium with the regional climate (the **monoclimax**).
*   **Gleasonian View:** Succession is not predictable. The "endpoint" is contingent on history, chance dispersal events, and ongoing disturbances (**polyclimax** or no true climax).
*   **Modern View:** The Gleasonian view prevails. **Disturbance is natural and perpetual.**
    *   **Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis:** Highest diversity is maintained at intermediate levels of disturbance. Too much disturbance favors only pioneers; too little allows competitive dominants to exclude others.

---

### **Part 5: Classic Example: Succession in Glacier Bay, Alaska (20 min)**

*   **The Site:** A series of glaciers have been retreating since the late 1700s, exposing land for primary succession.
*   **The Pattern:**
    *   **0-10 years:** Bare rock, pioneer mosses and lichens, *Dryas* (a mat-forming shrub that fixes nitrogen).
    *   **20-30 years:** Alder (*Alnus*) thickets (also nitrogen-fixing), further enriching the soil.
    *   **50-100 years:** Sitka Spruce forest moves in, shaded by alders.
    *   **100-200 years:** Spruce dominates, a mixed climax forest develops.
*   **Why it's a classic:** It's a near-perfect natural laboratory showing the sequence of primary succession and the role of **facilitation** (e.g., nitrogen fixation by *Dryas* and Alder) in building the ecosystem.

### **Part 6: Wrap-up & Look Ahead (10 min)**

*   **Summary:** Succession is the engine of community change over time. It is driven by species interactions (facilitation, competition) and shaped by disturbance. The "climax" is often a shifting mosaic rather than a final state.
