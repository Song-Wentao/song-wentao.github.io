# **Lecture 08: Stability and Resilience**

**Course:** Community Ecology
**Instructor:** [-]

### **Part 1: Review & Defining Stability (15 min)**

*   **From Last Time:** Biodiversity can enhance ecosystem function (e.g., productivity).
*   **Today's Question:** How do communities respond to **perturbations**? Are diverse communities more stable?
*   **Key Definitions:**
    *   **Stability:** The ability of a community to resist change and maintain its structure/function.
    *   **Resistance:** The ability to remain unchanged during a disturbance.
    *   **Resilience:** The speed at which a community returns to its original state *after* a disturbance.
    *   **Alternative Stable States:** The concept that a community can exist in more than one stable configuration under the same environmental conditions.

---

### **Part 2: The Diversity-Stability Debate (25 min)**

*   **The Old Paradigm (1950s-60s):** More diverse communities are more stable. (Charles Elton)
*   **The Challenge (1970s):** Robert May's mathematical models showed that *more complex* model ecosystems (more species, more connections) were *less stable*. This created a major paradox.
*   **The Resolution (1990s-present):**
    *   May's models assumed random interactions.
    *   In nature, interactions are not random. **Structure matters.**
    *   **Key Findings from Experiments (like Cedar Creek):**
        *   **Insurance Hypothesis:** In a diverse community, if one species declines due to a disturbance, others with similar functions can compensate ("response diversity").
        *   **Overyielding:** Diverse communities are more productive, creating larger population sizes that are less likely to go extinct.
        *   **Asynchrony:** Species in diverse communities respond differently to environmental fluctuations, so the overall community property (e.g., total biomass) remains more constant.

### **Part 3: Alternative Stable States and Regime Shifts (25 min)**

*   A community can be "flipped" from one state to another by a disturbance that crosses a **threshold**.
*   **The ball-and-cup diagram:** The cup is a basin of attraction (a stable state). A push can move the ball (the community) over a hill into a new basin. It is hard to return.

#### **Classic Example: Coral Reef Phase Shift**
*   **State 1: Coral-Dominated**
    *   Healthy, complex habitat.
    *   Corals outcompete algae.
*   **Disturbance:** Overfishing of herbivorous fish, nutrient pollution, coral bleaching.
*   **Trigger:** A pulse event (e.g., a hurricane).
*   **State 2: Macroalgae-Dominated**
    *   Algae take over, shade and kill corals.
    *   The new state is stable and self-reinforcing (algae prevent coral recruitment).
*   **Hysteresis:** The path to reverse the shift is not the same as the path that caused it; it requires much more effort (e.g., drastic reduction in nutrients AND reintroduction of herbivores).

---

### **Part 4: Applying the Concepts: Resilience in Management (15 min)**

*   The goal of conservation is often to maintain **ecosystem resilience**.
*   **How to promote resilience?**
    1.  **Protect Biodiversity:** It provides functional redundancy and response diversity.
    2.  **Protect Keystone Species:** Their loss can disproportionately reduce stability.
    3.  **Maintain Connectivity:** Allows for re-colonization after local extinction.
    4.  **Reduce Multiple Stressors:** Systems are more likely to tip when facing several pressures simultaneously (e.g., warming, pollution, fragmentation).
*   **Example:** Managing lakes to prevent eutrophication. Once a lake flips to an algae-dominated, oxygen-poor state, it is very difficult and expensive to restore.

---

### **Part 5: Wrap-up & Look Ahead (10 min)**

*   **Summary:** Stability is a multi-faceted concept (resistance, resilience). While theoretical models were initially pessimistic, empirical evidence shows that biodiversity generally enhances stability. The existence of alternative stable states warns us that ecosystems can change abruptly and irreversibly.
